# Why nighttime phone use harms sleep: a review of the mechanisms

**Verification standard.** I confirmed every study cited below with a search result, a PubMed/Europe PMC abstract, or a fetched full text. I read full text for Bauducco 2024, Schöllhorn 2023, Duraccio 2021 and Yoo 2007. Items marked **[UNVERIFIED]** could not be confirmed from a primary source. N = sample size.

**Conflict-of-interest note.** Michael Gradisar co-authored several key sources here (Bauducco 2024, Hjetland 2025, Robbins 2025, King 2013). His affiliations are Sleep Cycle AB and WINK Sleep Pty Ltd. That is a commercial sleep-app company, which is relevant to Locturne. His group's conclusions still match independent labs (Rasch/Combertaldi in Switzerland, Duraccio in the US, Höhn in Austria).

---

## Bottom line

Your hunch holds. The best current synthesis is Bauducco, Pillion, Bartel, Reynolds, Kahn & Gradisar (2024), *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 76:101933, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101933 (open access). It looked at the four classic mechanisms: bright light, arousal, nighttime disruption and sleep displacement.
- **Light and arousal:** well-designed lab studies consistently find they delay falling asleep by **less than 10 minutes**. The largest effects are 9.9 min for light and 8.5 min for arousal.
- **Displacement and night-time interruption:** "may each reduce the sleep opportunity by an hour or more." These are also the least-studied mechanisms.
- **Light is not zero.** It reliably suppresses melatonin and shifts the body clock later by 30–90 min. It just doesn't translate into much trouble falling asleep that night.

## 1. Light and melatonin suppression

**The claim.** Screen light, especially blue light (which drives the eye's melanopsin receptors), suppresses melatonin, makes you more alert, and shifts the body clock later. That makes sleep harder.

### Studies supporting it

- **Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015), *PNAS* 112:1232–1237**, doi:10.1073/pnas.1418490112
  - Design: inpatient crossover, N=12. Participants read on an iPad for 4 h before bed (6–10 p.m.) for 5 consecutive nights, versus a printed book.
  - iPad reading brought longer time to fall asleep, less evening sleepiness, less melatonin, a later body clock, less REM sleep, and lower alertness next morning.
  - Sizes: sleep latency (time to fall asleep) was **+9.9 min** (per the Bauducco table; Penn State press release says "nearly 10 minutes"). The clock shifted **~90 min** later (dim-light melatonin onset, DLMO; Bauducco says 90 min, the BWH press release says "more than an hour").
  - Exact lux at the eye: **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- **Chinoy, Duffy & Czeisler (2018), *Physiological Reports***, doi:10.14814/phy2.13692
  - N=9, 5 evenings of unrestricted tablet use versus print, with self-chosen bedtimes.
  - Bedtime was ~31 min later (22:03 vs 21:32) and sleep onset later (22:25 vs 21:54).
  - Melatonin suppression was 54% vs 10%, and melatonin onset came ~48 min later.
  - Note that the later bedtime is itself partly displacement.
- **Gooley et al. (2011), *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 96:E463**, doi:10.1210/jc.2010-2098
  - N=116. Ordinary room light (<200 lux) versus dim light (<3 lux) in the 8 h before bed.
  - Melatonin onset came later in 99% of people, and melatonin duration was ~90 min shorter.
  - This is about **room lighting**, not phones.
- **Wood, Rea, Plitnick & Figueiro (2013), *Applied Ergonomics* 44:237**, doi:10.1016/j.apergo.2012.07.008
  - N=13, tablets at maximum brightness.
  - After 1 h, melatonin suppression was **not significantly different from zero**. It became significant only after 2 h.
- **Phillips et al. (2019), *PNAS* 116:12019**, doi:10.1073/pnas.1901824116
  - N=55, evening light at 10–2,000 lux for 5 h.
  - The light level that halves melatonin averaged **24.6 lux**. Individuals ranged from 6 to 350 lux, a more than 50-fold spread.
  - 10, 30 and 50 lux pushed melatonin onset later by 22, 77 and 109 min.
  - Caveat: this was whole-room light for 5 h, not a small handheld screen.
- **Schöllhorn, Stefani, Lucas, Spitschan, Slawik & Cajochen (2023), *Communications Biology* 6:228**, doi:10.1038/s42003-023-04598-4
  - N=72 males, 3.5 h of exposure starting 4 h before habitual bedtime. The display kept brightness and colour the same while varying only the blue, melanopsin-stimulating content.
  - Less of that content gave faster sleep onset, less melatonin suppression and a clock ~0.5 h earlier, with dose-dependent effects.
  - But sleep latency differed significantly **only at the brightest (computer-level) intensity**, not at phone-level intensity.
  - The paper cites typical evening screen levels: **smartphone ~20 lx, tablet ~40 lx, computer ~80 lx, TV ~10 lx.**
- **Trebucq … de la Iglesia, Casiraghi (2026), *Sleep***, doi:10.1093/sleep/zsag039
  - A natural experiment in an Indigenous Toba/Qom community where smartphones arrived recently. N=100, 8,407 nights of wrist-tracked sleep.
  - Smartphone users fell asleep **13 min later**, slept **22 min less**, and had melatonin onset **34 min later**.
  - The strongest real-world evidence that a clock shift happens, but the delay can't be separated into light versus behaviour.

### Counter-evidence

- **Bauducco et al. 2024, Table 1.** Across 11 studies comparing bright screens with controls, the difference in time to fall asleep ranged from **−3.9 to +9.9 min**, and several were negative.
  - Heath 2014: +3.3 min. van der Lely 2015: −1.9 min. Rångtell 2016: +0.4 min. Duraccio 2021: +0.5 min.
  - The authors call melatonin "uncoupled" from how quickly people fall asleep. They also note that **daylight earlier in the day blunts the effect of evening light**.
- **Duraccio, Zaugg, Blackburn & Jensen (2021), *Sleep Health***, doi:10.1016/j.sleh.2021.03.005
  - N=167, aged 18–24, randomised for 7 nights to Night Shift on, Night Shift off, or no phone. Sleep was measured with wrist trackers.
  - **No differences between the three groups.** Only in an exploratory subgroup (people sleeping more than 6.8 h) did no-phone beat Night Shift on sleep efficiency (p=.014) and time awake after first falling asleep (p=.013).
  - The authors conclude that something other than light, such as stimulating content, must be responsible.
- **Höhn et al. (2024), *Brain Communications***, doi:10.1093/braincomms/fcae173
  - N=68 males; smartphone with blue filter, smartphone without, or a book.
  - Melatonin was suppressed in both age groups. Adolescents recovered within 50 min.
  - Adults showed only reduced deep (N3) sleep in the first quarter of the night. Memory consolidation was unaffected.
- **Blue-blocking products don't help much:**
  - Cochrane review, Singh et al. 2023 (doi:10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2): 17 trials. It found no demonstrated sleep benefit, with low-certainty evidence. I have the abstract only; I didn't open its detailed sleep outcome.
  - Luna-Rangel 2025 meta-analysis (*Front Neurol*): 3 randomised trials, N=49. Time to fall asleep shifted −4.9 min (not significant).
- **Vézina-Im et al. (2025), *Sleep Health***, doi:10.1016/j.sleh.2025.06.010
  - N=1,342, a nationally representative Canadian survey.
  - Both occasional and regular bedtime screen users reported the best sleep health, which does not fit a simple dose-response.

### Verdict: real biology, small sleep effect (moderate importance)

- Melatonin suppression and a 30–90 min clock delay are well replicated.
- The effect on falling asleep that night is small, under 10 min.
- A phone at ~20 lx is below most lab doses, though near the average halving point in Phillips. Individual sensitivity varies more than 50-fold.
- Light probably matters most for night owls and people with delayed sleep phase, for long sessions in a dark room, and through the cumulative clock delay rather than any single night.
- Rank: **4th–5th**.

## 2. Displacement: phone time replaces sleep time

**The claim.** Time on the phone pushes bedtime, and then "shut-eye" time, later. Wake time is fixed by alarms, so total sleep shrinks.

### Evidence

- **Bauducco 2024:**
  - In gaming studies with self-selected bedtimes, adolescents went to bed **up to 1 h 15 min later**.
  - Players high versus low in "flow" (absorption in the game) differed by **~90 min** in bedtime.
  - Each 1-unit drop in perceived risk-taking consequences meant a **38-min later bedtime** (Reynolds et al., *Sleep Medicine* 2016 and *Acta Paediatrica* 2015; I only saw these via Bauducco and search snippets).
  - The authors conclude that displacement is the stronger explanation.
- **Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2017), *J Sleep Research***, doi:10.1111/jsr.12510
  - N=338 adults aged 18–25.
  - The average gap between getting into bed and trying to sleep ("shut-eye latency") was **39 min**.
  - A gap of more than 1 h meant **9.3× higher odds** of being a poor sleeper.
  - This is the two-step displacement model: first bedtime is delayed, then shut-eye time.
- **Hjetland, Skogen, Hysing, Gradisar & Sivertsen (2025), *Frontiers in Psychiatry***, doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1548273
  - N=45,202 Norwegian students.
  - Each extra hour of screen use after getting into bed meant **59% higher odds of insomnia symptoms** and **24 min less sleep**.
  - Social media was no worse than other activities. The authors read this as time displacement rather than content. It is cross-sectional.
- **Combertaldi et al. (2021), *Sleep Medicine***, doi:10.1016/j.sleep.2021.09.009
  - Lab study, N=32, blue light excluded.
  - 30 min of social media did not raise arousal or disturb sleep.
  - The authors say the main effect is "the additional time spent engaging in social media use at bedtime."
- **Carter et al. (2016), *JAMA Pediatrics***, doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341
  - Meta-analysis of 20 studies, N=125,198 aged 6–19.
  - Bedtime device use linked to inadequate sleep (OR 2.17), poor sleep quality (OR 1.46) and daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72).
  - Even access without use was linked to inadequate sleep (OR 1.79). All studies were cross-sectional.
- **Bedtime procrastination** (Kroese et al. 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00611; N=177): failing to go to bed at the intended time with no external reason, which predicted insufficient sleep. Bauducco reports an intervention that cut procrastination by 46 min.
- **Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022), *American Economic Review* 112(7):2424**, doi:10.1257/aer.20210867
  - Randomised experiment.
  - Social media is habit-forming: temporary incentives to use less had persistent effects.
  - Letting people set their own future limits substantially reduced use.
  - Their model attributes **31% of social media use to self-control problems**. This is the best causal evidence for "people use more than they want to," though it's not specific to night.

### Counter-evidence

- Reverse causation and the "time filler" idea (Bauducco 2024): people who can't sleep yet may pick up the phone, so that time would have been spent awake anyway. Daily diary studies show "trivial" day-to-day effects.
- Most displacement data are correlational. Direct experiments are few: mostly gaming studies with adolescents, plus the small randomised trials under section 3.

### Verdict: strongest-supported mechanism (#1)

- Effect sizes run from tens of minutes to more than an hour, versus under 10 min for light and arousal.
- It maps directly onto what an app can control: a hard stop at the intended bedtime.

## 3. Arousal from stimulating content

**The claim.** Exciting, emotional or mentally demanding content (gaming, social media, cliffhangers) raises heart rate and racing thoughts, which delays sleep.

### Evidence

- **Higuchi et al. (2005), *J Sleep Research***: N=7. Gaming raised heart rate, delayed sleep onset and cut REM sleep. A bright display did not affect sleep.
- **King et al. (2013), *J Sleep Research***, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2869.2012.01060.x
  - N=17 adolescents. 150 min versus 50 min of violent gaming.
  - Measured sleep: **−27 min total sleep, −7% sleep efficiency**. Self-reported time to fall asleep: +17 min.
  - **Heart rate did not differ.** The authors: "physiological arousal may not necessarily be the mechanism." Duration drove the effect.
- **Levenson et al. (2017), *Sleep***, doi:10.1093/sleep/zsx113: N=1,763 US young adults. Checking social media often in the 30 min before bed meant adjusted odds of 1.62 for sleep disturbance, independent of total use.
- **Woods & Scott (2016), *J Adolescence***, doi:10.1016/j.adolescence.2016.05.008: N=467. Night-time social media use and emotional investment in it were linked to poorer sleep quality.
- **Randomised restriction trials (they don't separate arousal from displacement):**
  - He et al. 2020, *PLOS ONE* (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0228756): N=38, no phone for 30 min before bed for 4 weeks. Faster sleep onset, longer sleep, less pre-sleep arousal. Small pilot; outcomes by diary and questionnaire.
  - Tu et al. 2023, *Addictive Behaviors*: N=152 students with problematic phone use. Restricting phone use in bed improved sleep quality, mediated by lower pre-sleep **cognitive** arousal.

### Counter-evidence

- **Bauducco Table 2:** seven lab studies of arousing content added only **−5.3 to +8.5 min** to time to fall asleep, and heart-rate effects were inconsistent.
- **Combertaldi 2021:** social media did not raise heart rate or heart-rate variability.
- **Baselgia 2023** (via Bauducco): a cliffhanger raised pre-sleep arousal, yet that group fell asleep ~5 min *faster*.

### Verdict: moderate at most (#3–4)

- Lab-induced arousal barely moves sleep onset.
- Worry and emotional investment in social media may matter more than raw excitement.
- Much of what looks like arousal is probably time spent, i.e. displacement.

## 4. Notifications, night awakenings and "vamping"

**The claim.** A phone in the bedroom wakes you, or you wake and check it, which fragments sleep. "Vamping" is slang for staying up late on devices.

### Evidence

- **Van den Bulck (2003), *J Sleep Research***, doi:10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00362.x: 43% of adolescents were woken by texts at least monthly, and 11% weekly. I got these figures via Bauducco; the abstract is not indexed.
- **Van den Bulck (2007), *Sleep* 30:1220**, doi:10.1093/sleep/30.9.1220
  - Prospective, N=1,656 adolescents, 1-year follow-up.
  - Using the phone after lights out more than once a week meant **OR 5.1** for being very tired a year later. Use between midnight and 3 a.m. meant OR 3.9.
  - 35% of "very tired" cases were attributed to phone use. Outcomes were self-reported.
- **Rod et al. (2018), *PLOS ONE***, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0204811
  - N=815 young adults, more than 250,000 **objectively logged** phone actions.
  - 12% used the phone 3–5 h after bedtime, and 41% had phone-interrupted sleep on at least one weekday in 4 weeks.
  - Frequent interrupters reported **48 min shorter** sleep.
- **Dissing et al. (2022), *Scientific Reports*** (same cohort), doi:10.1038/s41598-022-10116-z: small links with stress and depression at baseline that **did not replicate at 4-month follow-up**.
- **Thomée et al. (2011), *BMC Public Health***: N=4,156 aged 20–24, prospective. Feeling stressed by being constantly reachable predicted sleep disturbances a year later.
- **Munezawa et al. (2011), *Sleep***: N=94,777 Japanese adolescents. Calling or texting after lights out was linked to sleep disturbances, with small effect sizes.
- **Bauducco 2024:** among more than 10,000 adolescents, leaving the ringer on was linked to more sleep problems. Phone-related night awakenings meant >3× odds of trouble falling asleep a year later.
- **Carter 2016:** access alone in the bedroom meant OR 1.79 for inadequate sleep.

### Against or limits

- It is understudied, with no randomised trial of phone-out-of-bedroom and measured sleep that I could find.
- A recent Turkish validation of a "vamping" scale (Yigit et al. 2026) is correlational only.

### Verdict: probably large but under-tested (#2)

- Bauducco calls it "a promising lead… with a potentially large influence."
- Do Not Disturb or flight mode, and a phone out of reach, are cheap fixes.

## 5. Reinforcement design, habit, FoMO and self-control

- **Reward learning is real.** Lindström et al. (2021), *Nature Communications* 12:1311 (doi:10.1038/s41467-020-19607-x) analysed more than 1M posts from over 4,000 users. People timed their posts according to reinforcement-learning principles, posting more when likes came faster.
  - **No human study directly shows "dopamine hits" from scrolling.** "Dopamine" claims are inferred from animal and reward-learning work.
- **Habits.**
  - Wood & Neal (2007), *Psychological Review* (doi:10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843): cues in the environment trigger habitual responses without an intervening goal. This supports changing the context, e.g. removing cue access at bedtime.
  - Oulasvirta et al. (2012), *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 16:105: "checking habits," brief repeated checks for quickly available rewards. Users experienced them as annoyance rather than addiction.
  - Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40:998: N=96. Median ~66 days to reach automaticity, ranging from 18 to 254 days.
- **Design features.**
  - Lukoff et al. (2021, CHI; doi:10.1145/3411764.3445467): N=120 survey plus 13 co-design sessions. Autoplay and recommendations undermine users' sense of control; playlists and search support it.
  - Rixen et al. (2023, *Proc ACM HCI* 7:228): 1-week field study, N=46. Infinite scroll creates a regretful "loop," and people mostly break it for **outside-context reasons** (something else demands attention). That supports external stopping cues, which is Locturne's model.
  - These are all qualitative or small studies.
- **FoMO (fear of missing out).** Przybylski, Murayama, DeHaan & Gladwell (2013), *Computers in Human Behavior* 29:1841: FoMO linked to higher social media engagement and lower mood and need satisfaction. Bauducco cites a study of more than 3,000 adolescents linking high FoMO to later sleep onset and shorter sleep.
- **Self-control and ego depletion — contested.**
  - Hagger et al. (2016), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11:546: 23 labs, N=2,141, **d=0.04** with a confidence interval including zero.
  - Vohs et al. (2021), *Psychological Science*: 36 sites, N=3,531, **d=0.06**, and the data were 4× more likely under the null hypothesis.
  - Don't claim willpower "runs out" by night. *Trait* self-control does predict bedtime procrastination (Kroese 2014; Exelmans & Van den Bulck, via Bauducco).
- **Decision fatigue.** The famous hungry-judges study (Danziger et al. 2011, *PNAS*) is disputed: Weinshall-Margel & Shapard argued the non-random order of cases explains it. Don't use it.
- **"Brain drain" (phone presence reduces cognitive capacity).** Ward et al. 2017 **failed to replicate** in a preregistered direct replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022, *Acta Psychologica*).

### Verdict

- This is best understood as the **engine of displacement**: habit, self-control problems, autoplay and FoMO explain *why* people overshoot bedtime.
- The habit and self-control findings are good (Allcott 2022, Wood & Neal).
- Neuro-marketing claims about dopamine, ego depletion and decision fatigue are weak or failed replication. Avoid them in copy.

## 6. Circadian misalignment and social jetlag

- **Wittmann, Dinich, Merrow & Roenneberg (2006), *Chronobiology International* 23:497**, doi:10.1080/07420520500545979: N=501. Social jetlag is the mismatch between body-clock time and social schedule. It was linked to lower wellbeing and more stimulant use, especially smoking.
- **Roenneberg et al. (2012), *Current Biology* 22:939**, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038: social jetlag linked to higher BMI beyond sleep duration. The "~33% higher odds of overweight per hour" figure is from secondary sources, **[UNVERIFIED]** in the abstract.
- **Wright et al. (2013), *Current Biology***: after camping under natural light only, participants' clocks shifted earlier to align with sunrise and sunset, with the biggest shift in night owls.
- **Meta-analyses (all cross-sectional; the phone-specific link is inferred):**
  - Social jetlag and depression in young people: OR 1.44 for jetlag of 2 h or more; r=0.16 (Sun et al. 2025, *BMC Psychiatry*).
  - Social jetlag and anxiety: Fisher's z=0.06, a tiny effect (Ravenhall 2026, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*).
  - Lu & Tsai 2026: certainty of evidence rated "very low."
  - Bouman 2024: high social jetlag meant +0.69 BMI.

### Verdict: real but indirect (#4–5)

Phones feed into this through both light (a 34–90 min clock delay) and late bedtimes. The health links are small and observational.

## 7. Morning phone use

This is **honestly thin.** I found **no peer-reviewed study** isolating "checking the phone immediately on waking" and its effects on mood, stress or cortisol. Popular claims about "cortisol spikes" and "dopamine hijacking" are **unsupported**.

What exists:
- **Snoozing** (related behaviour, often done on the phone):
  - Mattingly et al. (2022), *Sleep* (doi:10.1093/sleep/zsac184): 57% of N=450 snoozed. Snoozers had higher resting heart rate and lighter sleep before waking, but did not sleep less or feel sleepier.
  - Sundelin, Landry & Axelsson (2024), *J Sleep Research* (doi:10.1111/jsr.14054): survey N=1,732 plus lab study N=31. 30 min of snoozing lost ~6 min of sleep and **improved or did not affect** cognition. No effect on cortisol or mood.
  - Robbins … Gradisar, Czeisler (2025), *Scientific Reports*: 55.6% of more than 3M logged nights (from a sleep-tracking app) ended with a snooze, averaging 10.8 min. Snoozing was more likely after later-than-usual bedtimes.
  - A Japanese lab study (*J Physiol Anthropology* 2022) found snooze alarms worsened sleep inertia, the groggy period after waking. I did not verify its N or authors.
- **Morning light helps grogginess.** Didikoglu et al. (2023), *PNAS* (doi:10.1073/pnas.2301608120): N=59. Brighter light late in sleep and after waking was linked to less early-morning sleepiness. A phone in a dark bedroom delivers little light; getting up into daylight delivers a lot. That link to phones is my inference, not tested.
- **Rozman et al. (2026), *Chronobiology International***: N=174 athletes, correlational. Daily pre-sleep phone use was marginally linked to poorer morning mood.

### Verdict: weak / speculative

The defensible pitch is behavioural: in-bed scrolling delays getting up and getting daylight, and a snooze-scroll loop extends time in bed. Don't claim physiological harm.

## 8. What sleep loss itself does

- **Van Dongen et al. (2003), *Sleep* 26:117**, doi:10.1093/sleep/26.2.117
  - N=48; 4, 6 or 8 h in bed for 14 days, versus 0 h for 3 days.
  - 6 h or less per night gave cumulative deficits **equivalent to up to 2 nights of total deprivation**.
  - Subjective sleepiness plateaued, so **people don't notice** how impaired they are.
- **Yoo et al. (2007), *Current Biology* 17:R877**, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007
  - N=26 (14 sleep-deprived, 12 controls), ~35 h awake.
  - **+60% amygdala response** to negative pictures, and weaker prefrontal–amygdala connectivity. Small N.
  - Broader support: Palmer et al. (2024), *Psychological Bulletin* (doi:10.1037/bul0000410), 154 studies, N=5,717. Sleep loss reduces positive mood (effect size −0.27 to −1.14) and increases anxiety (0.57–0.63).
- **Immune.** Prather et al. (2015), *Sleep* (doi:10.5665/sleep.4968): N=164 given cold virus. Sleeping less than 5 h meant **OR 4.50** for catching a cold (confidence interval 1.08–18.69, very wide). 5–6 h meant OR 4.24 versus more than 7 h.
- **Metabolic.**
  - Spiegel, Leproult & Van Cauter (1999), *Lancet* (doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8): N=11 men, 4 h in bed for 6 nights. Worse glucose tolerance, higher evening cortisol, more sympathetic nervous activity.
  - Tasali et al. (2022), *JAMA Internal Medicine*, randomised trial N=80: sleeping ~1.2 h longer led to **−270 kcal/day** intake.
- **Mortality.** Cappuccio et al. (2010), *Sleep* (doi:10.1093/sleep/33.5.585): 16 studies, N=1.38M. Short sleep RR 1.12; **long sleep RR 1.30**. It's a U-curve, largely confounded (long sleep often signals illness), and sleep was self-reported.
- **Walker caveat.**
  - Guzey (2019), "Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep* Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors" (guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep): documents that the WHO never declared a "sleep loss epidemic," that "shorter sleep = shorter life" ignores the U-curve, that the cancer-doubling claim is contradicted by a 2018 meta-analysis, that a data bar was dropped from an injury graph, and that some percentages are impossible.
  - Guzey's page states a related Walker paper was retracted; I haven't checked that independently.
  - Bauducco 2024 notes Walker amplified the Chang light study and later toned down that message on podcasts.
  - Cite primary studies, not *Why We Sleep*.

### Verdict

Consequences of chronic restriction (cognitive, emotional, immune, metabolic) are well supported experimentally. Mortality claims are observational and U-shaped, so don't overstate them.

## Which mechanisms matter most, per the evidence

| Rank | Mechanism | Typical effect size | Evidence quality | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Displacement / bedtime procrastination** (driven by #5) | 24 min less sleep per in-bed screen hour (Hjetland); up to 75–90 min later bedtimes (gaming experiments); 39 min average shut-eye gap (Exelmans) | Large observational datasets plus a few experiments; causal evidence for self-control problems (Allcott) | **Strongest** |
| 2 | **Night-time notifications and checking** | 48 min shorter sleep in frequent interrupters (Rod, logged data); OR 3–5 for later tiredness (Van den Bulck) | Observational, some objective logs; no randomised trials | Likely large, understudied |
| 3 | **Habit, reinforcement and self-control** (the *cause* of #1 and #2) | 31% of use from self-control problems (Allcott) | Good for habit and self-control; dopamine talk speculative; ego depletion failed replication | Real driver; avoid neuro-hype |
| 4 | **Arousal from content** | −5 to +8.5 min to fall asleep; heart rate inconsistent | Many well-designed but small lab studies | Small |
| 5 | **Light and melatonin** | Melatonin clearly suppressed; clock 30–90 min later; falling asleep −4 to +10 min | Strongest lab physiology, weakest translation to sleep; Night Shift and blue-blockers show no benefit | Real biology, small sleep effect; matters for sensitive night owls |
| 6 | **Circadian misalignment / social jetlag** | ~34 min clock delay in users (Toba/Qom); small health correlations | Natural experiment plus cross-sectional meta-analyses | Indirect, modest |
| 7 | **Morning phone use** | No direct data; snoozing loses ~6 min and is roughly neutral | Very thin | Speculative |
| — | **Downstream: sleep loss itself** | Cognitive deficits equal to 1–2 nights' total deprivation (Van Dongen); cold OR ~4.5; −270 kcal/day with more sleep | Strong experimental evidence | Why any of this matters |

**Implication for Locturne.** The evidence favours exactly what an app blocker can do:
1. Enforce the intended bedtime, cutting displacement and procrastination.
2. Silence and remove the phone overnight, cutting notifications and checking.
3. Break the habit loop.

Blue-light messaging is the weakest scientific pitch. Morning-phone harm claims should stay behavioural ("get up and into daylight"), not physiological.

### Unverified items
- Chang 2015's lux values.
- The results of the Cajochen et al. 2022 meta-analysis of evening light and lab-measured sleep (*Lighting Research & Technology*, doi:10.1177/14771535221078765). I confirmed only that it included 21 studies; the abstract was paywalled.
- Roenneberg's "33% per hour" figure.
- The Walker retraction.
- The Japanese snooze study's authors and N.
- The Reynolds bedtime-delay numbers, which I saw only via Bauducco and search snippets.
