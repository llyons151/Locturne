# Evidence review: interventions for nighttime smartphone use, sleep, and the morning get-up (for Locturne)

**How claims are marked.** **[A]** means I checked it against the abstract (Europe PMC, the publisher or PubMed metadata). **[FT]** means I checked it against the full text (the PDF or PMC page I actually read). **[S]** means the number comes from secondary or search summaries only, so treat it as likely but not checked against the source. **[U]** means unverified. I have not invented any author, year or number. Where a number could not be confirmed I say so.

**Retracted, do not cite.** Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment," *Psychological Science*. The journal published a retraction in 2026 (retraction DOI 10.1177/09567976261488042). The SAGE page now carries the "RETRACTED:" prefix, and Ariely's own statement says the data could not be substantiated. Use Bryan, Karlan & Nelson (2010) and Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022) for commitment devices instead.

**Two corrections to the brief.**
1. "Digital Addiction" (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song) was published in the *American Economic Review* in 2022, not *Econometrica*.
2. Sundelin et al. on snoozing appeared online in 2023 but is formally *J Sleep Res* 2024;33(3):e14054.

## 1. Screen-time restriction RCTs and experiments

**Perrault, Bayer, … Sterpenich (2019).** "Reducing the use of screen electronic devices in the evening is associated with improved sleep and daytime vigilance in adolescents." *Sleep* 42(9):zsz125. DOI 10.1093/sleep/zsz125. [A]
- **Design:** Within-subject. Two-week baseline, then a 40-minute sleep-education workshop, then two weeks of "no screens after 9 pm" on school nights. Measured with actigraphy and daily questionnaires, ages 12–19. The bioRxiv version reports about 569 adolescents recruited [S]. There was no randomised control group.
- **Findings:** Less screen time after 9 pm correlated with earlier sleep onset and longer total sleep, which in turn predicted better daytime vigilance. The abstract gives no minute values, and this is a correlational within-subject design.
- **Implication:** A fixed evening screen curfew is plausible and shifts sleep earlier. Being associative, it is weaker evidence than it sounds.

**Bartel, Scheeren & Gradisar (2019).** "Altering adolescents' pre-bedtime phone use to achieve better sleep health." *Health Communication* 34(4):456–462. DOI 10.1080/10410236.2017.1422099. [A]
- **Design:** Within-subject, at home. One baseline week, then an individualised phone stop time one hour before bed for one school week. N = 63, ages 14–18, 17% male.
- **Findings:** Phone use stopped 80 minutes earlier, lights went out 17 minutes earlier, and sleep was 21 minutes longer (all p ≤ .01).
- **Key caveat:** Only 26% of those approached agreed to take part, which the authors read as low motivation to change evening phone use.
- **Implication:** A one-hour pre-bed cut-off buys about 20 minutes of sleep. The hard problem is getting people to opt in, which an app that people download voluntarily solves by self-selection.

**He, Tu, Xiao, Su & Tang (2020).** "Effect of restricting bedtime mobile phone use on sleep, arousal, mood, and working memory: A randomized pilot trial." *PLOS ONE* 15(2):e0228756. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0228756. [A]/[FT]
- **Design:** Randomised pilot. N = 38 (19 per arm), Chinese university students with poor sleep. The intervention was "no phone for 30 minutes before bed" for four weeks.
- **Findings:** Sleep latency, sleep duration, PSQI score, pre-sleep arousal, positive affect and n-back working memory all improved. The full-text summary gives latency about 12 minutes shorter and sleep about 18 minutes longer [FT, via summariser].
- **Limitations:** Self-report only (diary and PSQI), small sample, motivated volunteers.
- **Implication:** Even a 30-minute pre-bed buffer shows benefit. A "wind-down" block that starts before bedtime has pilot RCT support.

**Hughes & Burke (2018).** "Sleeping with the frenemy: How restricting 'bedroom use' of smartphones impacts happiness and wellbeing." *Computers in Human Behavior* 85:236–244. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218301523 [S]
- **Design:** N = 95, mostly millennials. Control versus one week of no phone use in the bedroom.
- **Findings:** Happiness and quality of life improved significantly. The sleep benefits ("easier to fall asleep, better sleep quality") appear to be partly qualitative or anecdotal [S; I could not verify full-text statistics].
- **Implication:** "Phone out of the bedroom" is a stimulus-control move with small-trial support for wellbeing. Treat the sleep claims as soft.

**Hunt, Marx, Lipson & Young (2018).** "No More FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression." *J Social & Clinical Psychology* 37(10):751–768. DOI 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751. [S/A]
- **Design:** N = 143 undergraduates. Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat limited to 10 minutes each per day (about 30 minutes total) versus usual use, for three weeks.
- **Findings:** Loneliness and depression fell in the limited group. Anxiety and FOMO fell in both groups, which the authors attribute to self-monitoring.
- **Implication:** Limiting specific apps rather than the whole phone is enough to move wellbeing. Self-monitoring alone has some effect.

**Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow (2020).** "The welfare effects of social media." *AER* 110(3):629–676. DOI 10.1257/aer.20190658. [A]
- **Design:** RCT. Four weeks of Facebook deactivation before the 2018 US midterms. Sample size about 2,700 recruited [U].
- **Findings:** Offline activity rose, subjective wellbeing improved, and there was a large, persistent drop in Facebook use after the experiment ended. Valuations of Facebook also fell.
- **Implication:** Forced abstinence produces lasting reductions in use, which is evidence that the habit decays. Blocking can change behaviour beyond the blocked period.

**Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022).** "Digital Addiction." *AER* 112(7):2424–2463. DOI 10.1257/aer.20210867. [FT]
- **Design:** Factorial RCT, N = 2,126 analysed. Two treatments:
  - **Bonus arm:** paid to reduce use of six apps (Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, Snapchat, browser, YouTube).
  - **Limit arm:** self-set daily app limits in the Phone Dashboard app that "could not be immediately overridden." A "snooze" option was randomised to delays of 0, 2, 5 or 20 minutes, or disabled. About 4% of users opted out of limits permanently.
- **Findings:**
  - The limit arm cut use by 22 minutes a day (16%) over 12 weeks, still 19 minutes a day in the last week.
  - 78% of participants set binding limits with no incentive to do so, and valued the limit feature at about $4.20 per three weeks.
  - The bonus cut use by 56 minutes a day (39%) while it ran, and 19 then 12 minutes a day in later three-week periods, which indicates habit formation.
  - People underestimated their own future use, which the authors interpret as projection bias.
  - Their model attributes 31% of social media use to self-control problems. (The "22%" in the brief is the limit effect in minutes, not a share.)
  - Results on the snooze delays were deferred to a separate paper, which I could not find.
- **Implication:** This is the strongest direct evidence that a self-chosen, hard-to-override app limit reduces use durably and that people want it. It is close to the core evidence for Locturne's model.

**Brailovskaia, Delveaux, John, … Margraf (2023, online 2022).** "Finding the 'sweet spot' of smartphone use: Reduction or abstinence…" *J Exp Psychol: Applied*. DOI 10.1037/xap0000430. [A]
- **Design:** N = 619 in Germany. Seven days of full abstinence (n = 200), versus one hour less per day (n = 226), versus control (n = 193). Follow-ups at one and four months. Self-report measures.
- **Findings:** Both interventions improved depression, anxiety, life satisfaction and physical activity. Effects were stronger and more stable in the reduction group than the abstinence group. Press materials report about 45 minutes a day less use at four months [S].
- **Implication:** Partial, sustainable reduction beats total abstinence. That supports blocking chosen apps at night rather than the whole phone all day.

**Castelo, Kushlev, Ward, Esterman & Reiner (2025).** "Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being." *PNAS Nexus* 4(2):pgaf017. DOI 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf017. [A]/[FT]
- **Design:** RCT with delayed-intervention crossover. N = 467 iPhone users. The Freedom app blocked all mobile internet for two weeks.
- **Findings from the full text:**
  - Only 25.5% met the preregistered compliance threshold (at least 10 of 14 days); the analyses are intention-to-treat.
  - Effect sizes (Cohen's dz): sustained attention 0.24, mental-health symptoms 0.57, wellbeing 0.46.
  - Sleep rose about 17 minutes a night (dz 0.14).
  - 91% improved on at least one outcome [A]. Benefits persisted after the block ended.
- **Implication:** A strong, recent RCT using an iOS blocking app. The low compliance is a warning that even motivated users bail out of total blocks, so a scoped, time-boxed block is more sustainable.

**Hartstein, Mathew, Reichenberger, Rodriguez, … Hale (2024).** "The impact of screen use on sleep health across the lifespan: A National Sleep Foundation consensus statement." *Sleep Health*. DOI 10.1016/j.sleh.2024.05.001. [A]
- **Design:** Modified Delphi/RAND with a 16-member panel. Reviewed 522 empirical and 35 experimental studies.
- **Consensus reached (5 of 10 statements):**
  - Screen use impairs sleep in children and adolescents.
  - Pre-bed content impairs sleep in children and adolescents.
  - Behavioural strategies may attenuate the harm.
- **No consensus:** on whether pre-bed screen light impairs sleep at any age, or on adults generally.
- **Implication:** Target engaging content (feeds, games), not light. Blue-light filters and Night Shift are not the lever. For adults the evidence is thinner, so avoid overclaiming in marketing.

**Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015).** "Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness." *PNAS* 112(4):1232–1237. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1418490112. [A]
- **Design:** Laboratory crossover, eReader versus printed book. Sample size not confirmed.
- **Findings:** Longer sleep latency, suppressed and delayed melatonin, less REM sleep, and lower alertness the next morning.
- **Implication:** A lab-level mechanism under extreme exposure conditions. Real-world phone light effects are small (see Hartstein).

**Supporting correlational papers**
- **Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016).** "Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults." *Soc Sci Med* 148:93–101. Cross-sectional, N = 844. Bedtime phone use was associated with worse sleep, later rise time and fatigue. [S]
- **Kroese, De Ridder, Evers & Adriaanse (2014).** "Bedtime procrastination." *Front Psychol* 5:611. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00611. Coined bedtime procrastination: going to bed later than intended with no external reason. It mediates the link between low self-regulation and insufficient sleep. [S]
- **Implication:** The target behaviour is a self-regulation failure, which is why commitment devices fit.

## 2. App-based blockers, friction and commitment devices

**Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023).** "Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec." *PNAS* 120(8):e2213114120. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2213114120. [FT]
- **Field study:** Observational, N = 280 who completed six weeks out of 719. When a target app was opened, one sec showed a deliberation message, a 10-second delay and a "dismiss" option.
  - 36% of open attempts were dismissed.
  - Open attempts fell 37% between week 1 and week 6.
  - Combined, actual opens fell 57%.
- **Controlled experiment:** N = 500. The **dismiss option** drove the effect. The deliberation message alone had no significant effect.
- **Major limitation:** Self-selection. People who dropped out saw their use as less problematic.
- **Implication:** For moments of override, pair a short delay with an easy "never mind" exit. Messages alone do little.

**Haliburton, Grüning, Riedel, Schmidt & Terzimehić (2024).** "A longitudinal in-the-wild investigation of design frictions to prevent smartphone overuse." *CHI '24*. DOI 10.1145/3613904.3642370. [S]
- **Design:** N = 1,039 one sec users, average 13.4 weeks, plus a survey of 249.
- **Findings:** Users mostly targeted social media. Friction reduced open attempts over time and made the remaining opens more intentional.
- **Implication:** Brief friction keeps working over roughly three months. It is a plausible "soft" layer around a hard block.

**Kim, Jung, Ko & Lee (2019).** "GoalKeeper: Exploring interaction lockout mechanisms for regulating smartphone use." *Proc ACM IMWUT* 3(1):16. DOI 10.1145/3314403. [FT]
- **Design:** Four-week within-subject field experiment, N = 36. Three lockout levels:
  - **Non-lockout:** a warning only.
  - **Weak lockout:** escalating 1, 5, 15, 30 and 60-minute locks.
  - **Strong lockout:** locked until midnight.
- **Reduction in daily use versus baseline:**

| Lockout | Minutes/day saved | Statistics |
|---|---|---|
| Non-lockout | 32.1 | not significant (p = .091, d = 0.21) |
| Weak | 50.4 | p = .008, d = 0.35 |
| Strong | 73.7 | p = .001, d = 0.54 |

- **Costs of the strong lockout:**
  - Highest negative emotion or stress, rated 51.5/100 versus 36.2 for the warning (p = .017).
  - Participants set looser goals "just in case," and 20 of 36 raised their limits during the strong condition.
- **Preferences:** 52.8% preferred the weak lockout, 33.3% the strong, 13.9% the warning.
- **Other finding:** 92% of participants frequently ignored the warning.
- **Implication:** This is the clearest data on the strict-versus-flexible trade-off. Stronger locks reduce use more but cause more stress and goal inflation. Most users prefer a middle option.

**Kovacs, Wu & Bernstein (2018).** "Rotating online behavior change interventions increases effectiveness but also increases attrition." *Proc ACM HCI* 2(CSCW):95. DOI 10.1145/3274364. [FT]
- **Design:** Three in-the-wild HabitLab experiments.
- **Findings:** Static interventions lose effect as users habituate. Rotating interventions reduced time on site but increased uninstalls. A just-in-time explanation of the rotation halved attrition.
- **Implication:** Novelty helps effectiveness but hurts retention. Explain any change before it happens.

**Kovacs, Wu & Bernstein (2021).** "Not now, ask later: Users weaken their behavior change regimen over time, but believe they will choose stronger ones." *CHI '21*. arXiv 2101.11743. [A]
- **Design:** 8,000+ HabitLab users.
- **Findings:** Users start with hard interventions and drift to easier ones. They keep expecting to return to hard settings soon and decline to save the easier preference.
- **Implication:** Making it easy to lower strictness in the moment will be used. Lock settings changes for the night once the block has started, and make changes take effect tomorrow (Phone Dashboard did the same).

**Monge Roffarello & De Russis (2019).** "The race towards digital wellbeing: Issues and opportunities." *CHI '19*. DOI 10.1145/3290605.3300616. [S]
- **Design:** Reviewed 42 apps and 1,128 user reviews, plus a three-week field study of the "Socialize" app with N = 38.
- **Findings:** The tools help in specific situations but do not build new habits, and users see them as not restrictive enough.
- **Implication:** Users themselves ask for more teeth than stock Screen Time provides. That supports Locturne's positioning.

**Monge Roffarello & De Russis (2023).** "Achieving digital wellbeing through digital self-control tools: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *ACM TOCHI* 30(4):53. DOI 10.1145/3571810. [S]
- I could not access the pooled effect sizes. Cite it for the landscape, not for numbers.

**Lyngs, Lukoff, Slovak, … Shadbolt (2019).** "Self-control in cyberspace: Applying dual systems theory to a review of digital self-control tools." *CHI '19*. https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00157 [S]
- Reviewed 367 apps and extensions. Found four core functions: blocking, self-tracking, goal advancement, and reward/punishment. Blocking dominates.
- **Implication:** A useful taxonomy for positioning. Few tools target a specific time window such as sleep.

**Grayscale and bundled nudges**
- **Zimmermann & Sobolev (2023).** "Digital strategies for screen time reduction: A randomized field experiment." *Cyberpsychol Behav Soc Netw*. DOI 10.1089/cyber.2022.0027. [A] Preregistered, N = 112. Grayscale (friction) gave an immediate, significant cut in objectively measured screen time. Goal-setting (time limits) gave a smaller, gradual reduction. There was no immediate effect on wellbeing or grades.
- **Dekker & Baumgartner (2023/2024).** "Is life brighter when your phone is not?" *Mobile Media & Communication*. DOI 10.1177/20501579231212062. [S] N = 84, one week of grayscale. Screen time fell about 20 minutes a day, but **unlocks did not change**. Perceived control improved; sleep quality did not.
- **Holte & Ferraro (2020/2023).** "True colors: Grayscale setting reduces screen time in college students." *Social Science Journal* 60(2). DOI 10.1080/03623319.2020.1737461. [S] About 38 minutes a day less.
- **Olson, Sandra, Chmoulevitch, Raz & Veissière (2022/2023).** "A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: RCT." *Int J Ment Health Addict*. DOI 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. [A] Study 2, N = 70, used a bundle of 10 user-chosen nudges (notifications off, grayscale, and so on). Compared with screen-time monitoring alone, it reduced problematic use and screen time and **improved sleep quality**.
- **Implication:** Grayscale during the wind-down or blocked window is a cheap, evidence-backed add-on. Letting users choose from a menu of strategies works.

**Wellspent RCT (2026).** "Promoting self-regulated social media use on smartphones with a mobile intervention app (Wellspent): Randomized controlled trial." *JMIR mHealth uHealth* 2026, e56824. https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e56824 [S]
- Three weeks, N = 70 iPhone users. A customisable, "moderately enforced" tool cut social-media screen time and perceived problematic use. Authors not verified.
- **Implication:** Recent iOS evidence that customisation plus moderate enforcement works in the short term.

**Commitment-device theory and evidence**
- **Bryan, Karlan & Nelson (2010).** "Commitment devices." *Annu Rev Econ* 2:671–698. DOI 10.1146/annurev.economics.102308.124324. [A] Reviews demand for commitment and its effectiveness. Distinguishes hard from soft commitments and argues soft ones help in practice.
- **Giné, Karlan & Zinman (2010).** "Put your money where your butt is: A commitment contract for smoking cessation." *AEJ: Applied* 2(4):213–235. DOI 10.1257/app.2.4.213. [S] Only 11% took up the offer, but those offered it were 3 percentage points more likely to pass a nicotine test at six months, and the effect persisted at 12 months.
- **Implication:** Demand for commitment is usually low, and Allcott's 78% take-up is unusually high. Most people who download a blocker are already the "sophisticated" minority.
- **Unverified:** I found no peer-reviewed study specifically on the effectiveness of Apple Screen Time or Downtime. Apple Community threads describe the "Ignore limit → all day" bypass, which is anecdotal. The general claim that flexible OS limits are easy to override is stated in Allcott et al. 2022 [FT].

## 3. Behaviour-change science

**Implementation intentions**
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006).** "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes." *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38:69–119. [S] 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 participants, d = 0.65 on goal attainment.
- **Implication:** Onboarding should capture if-then plans, for example "If it's 10:45 and I'm on my phone, I'll plug it in across the room."

**MCII (mental contrasting with implementation intentions) for bedtime procrastination**
- **Valshtein, Oettingen & Gollwitzer (2020).** "Using mental contrasting with implementation intentions to reduce bedtime procrastination: two randomised trials." *Psychology & Health* 35(3):275–301. DOI 10.1080/08870446.2019.1652753. [S] Undergraduates, N = 383 and N = 221. If-then plans for personal obstacles reduced bedtime procrastination at one and three weeks.
- **Implication:** Directly on target. A 3–5 minute MCII step in onboarding has trial support.

**Habit formation timelines**
- **Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010).** "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world." *Eur J Soc Psychol* 40:998–1009. DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674. [S]
  - 96 volunteers, 82 analysed, 12 weeks.
  - Median time to 95% of peak automaticity was **66 days**, with a range of **18–254 days**.
  - **A single missed day did not materially affect habit formation**; repeated misses did lower the ceiling.
  - About half had not reached habit-level automaticity by day 84.
- **Keller, Kwasnicka, Klaiber, Sichert, Lally & Fleig (2021).** "Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial." *Br J Health Psychol* 26:807–824. DOI 10.1111/bjhp.12504. [A] N = 192 over 84 days. Among those who formed habits, the median to peak automaticity was **59 days**. Routine-based and time-based cues worked equally well. Repeated enactment was the key predictor.
- **Implication:** Tell users "about two months," not "21 days." Never punish a single miss. The fixed bedtime (time cue) and getting out of bed (routine cue) are both valid anchors.

**Habit mechanics**
- **Wood & Rünger (2016).** "Psychology of habit." *Annu Rev Psychol* 67:289–314. DOI 10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417. [S]
- **Verplanken & Wood (2006).** "Interventions to break and create consumer habits." *J Public Policy & Marketing* 25:90–103. [S] Habit discontinuity: changes in context open a window for change.
- **Quinn, Pascoe, Wood & Neal (2010).** "Can't control yourself? Monitor those bad habits." *PSPB*. DOI 10.1177/0146167209360665. [S] Vigilant monitoring was the most effective self-control strategy for bad habits.
- **Implication:** Environmental restructuring (block, charger outside the bed) beats relying on willpower. Onboarding and fresh starts are the windows where change is easiest.

**Stimulus control (Bootzin)**
- **Bootzin (1972).** "Stimulus control treatment for insomnia." *Proc 80th Annual Convention APA*, 395–396. [S]
- **Jansson-Fröjmark et al. (2024).** "Stimulus control for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *J Sleep Res*. DOI 10.1111/jsr.14002. [S; findings not extracted]
- **Implication:** "The bed is for sleep" and "get up if you can't sleep" are core CBT-I components (see Furukawa below). Blocking stimulating apps in bed operationalises stimulus control.

**Temptation bundling**
- **Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014).** "Holding the Hunger Games hostage at the gym: An evaluation of temptation bundling." *Management Science* 60(2):283–299. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784. [FT via PMC summary]
  - Gym-only audiobook access raised gym visits 51% versus control; encouragement alone raised them 29%.
  - The effect decayed, especially after Thanksgiving.
  - 61% would pay for gym-only access afterwards.
- **Kirgios, Mandel, Park, Milkman, Gromet, Kay & Duckworth (2020).** "Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment." *OBHDP* 161:20–35. [S]
  - N = 6,792 at 24 Hour Fitness.
  - Weekly workouts rose 10–14%, and the effect persisted up to 17 weeks.
  - This is a much smaller effect at scale.
- **Implication:** A modest effect. Letting blocked apps "wake up" as a reward for the morning walk is a natural form of temptation bundling, but expect effects around 10%, not 50%.

**Fresh-start effect**
- **Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014).** "The fresh start effect: Temporal landmarks motivate aspirational behavior." *Management Science* 60(10):2563–2582. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901. [S] Archival data: searches for "diet," gym visits and goal commitments all rise after temporal landmarks such as a new week, month, birthday or holiday.
- **Implication:** Time re-engagement prompts and recovery after lapses to Mondays, month starts or birthdays.

**Self-monitoring**
- **Harkin, Webb, Chang, … Sheeran (2016).** "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence." *Psychol Bull* 142:198–229. DOI 10.1037/bul0000025. [S] 138 studies, N = 19,951, d ≈ 0.40. Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to others.
- **Implication:** Showing nights kept and mornings walked has good support. Keep it about behaviour, not sleep-stage scores (see orthosomnia).

**Streaks**
- **Silverman & Barasch (2023).** "On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions." *J Consumer Research* 49(6):1095–1117. DOI 10.1093/jcr/ucac029. [S]
  - Across seven studies, intact logged streaks increase subsequent engagement, and a **broken streak lowers it**, independent of actual past behaviour.
  - The drop is worse when people blame themselves.
  - It is **attenuated when the streak can be "repaired."**
- **Milkman et al. (2021).** "Megastudies improve the impact of applied behavioural science." *Nature* 600:478–483. DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-04128-4. [S]
  - 61,293 gym members, 54 interventions.
  - 45% of interventions raised visits 9–27%.
  - **The top intervention was a bonus for returning after a missed workout.**
  - Only 8% of interventions had significant effects after the four weeks.
- **Implication:** If Locturne uses streaks, it needs a repair or grace mechanism and a "welcome back" reward. Otherwise streaks work against the user through the abstinence-violation effect. Expect most engagement effects to fade after the programme.

**Loss aversion and financial incentives**
- **Patel, Asch, Rosin, … Volpp (2016).** "Framing financial incentives to increase physical activity among overweight and obese adults: A randomized, controlled trial." *Ann Intern Med* 164:385–394. DOI 10.7326/M15-1635. [S] Loss-framed incentives beat gain and lottery framing for a 7,000-steps-a-day goal. I could not verify the exact percentages of days the goal was met.
- **Implication:** Loss framing helps, but real money is out of scope, and GAME_PLAN rejects punishment mechanics. Use it only in soft forms, such as "keep your streak" with repair.

**Social accountability and gamification**
- **Patel, Small, Harrison, … (2019).** "Effectiveness of behaviorally designed gamification interventions with social incentives for increasing physical activity among overweight and obese adults across the United States: The STEP UP randomized clinical trial." *JAMA Intern Med* 179(12):1624–1632. [FT, abstract]
  - Increase over control during the 24-week intervention: competition +920 steps a day, support +689, collaboration +637.
  - At the 12-week follow-up: competition +569 (p = .009), support +428 (p = .04), collaboration +126 (not significant).
- **Implication:** Competition had the largest and most durable effect. For sleep, a light social layer (a buddy or a shared "we both kept bedtime" signal) is optional and should not be core.

## 4. Sleep interventions

**CBT-I overall**
- **Trauer, Qian, Doyle, Rajaratnam & Cunnington (2015).** "Cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Ann Intern Med* 163(3):191–204. DOI 10.7326/M14-2841. [A] 20 RCTs, N = 1,162.
  - Sleep onset latency improved 19.0 minutes.
  - Wake after sleep onset improved 26.0 minutes.
  - Total sleep time improved 7.6 minutes, not significant.
  - Sleep efficiency improved 9.9%.
  - Effects were sustained and there were no adverse outcomes.

**Which CBT-I components matter**
- **Furukawa, Sakata, … Perlis (2024).** "Components and delivery formats of cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic insomnia in adults: A systematic review and component network meta-analysis." *JAMA Psychiatry* 81(4):357–365. DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.5060. [A] 241 RCTs, N = 31,452. Remission incremental odds ratios (95% CI):

| Component | iOR | 95% CI | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cognitive restructuring | 1.68 | 1.28–2.20 | Effective |
| Third-wave components | 1.49 | 1.10–2.03 | Effective |
| Sleep restriction | 1.49 | 1.04–2.13 | Effective |
| Stimulus control | 1.43 | 1.00–2.05 | Effective |
| Sleep hygiene education | 1.01 | 0.77–1.32 | Not essential |
| Relaxation | 0.81 | 0.64–1.02 | Possibly counterproductive |

**Clinical guideline**
- **Edinger et al. (2021).** "Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an AASM clinical practice guideline." *J Clin Sleep Med* 17(2):255–262. DOI 10.5664/jcsm.8986. [S]
  - Strong recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I.
  - Conditional recommendations for stimulus control, sleep restriction and relaxation as single components.
  - Recommends **against** sleep hygiene as a stand-alone treatment.

**Sleep hygiene alone is weak**
- **Chung, Lee, Yeung, Chan, Chung & Lin (2018).** "Sleep hygiene education as a treatment of insomnia: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Family Practice* 35(4):365–375. [S] 13 RCTs. Small-to-medium pre-post improvements, about 5% sleep efficiency and about 2 PSQI points. Clearly inferior to CBT-I, and only subjective outcomes were significant. (The journal is *Family Practice*, not *Sleep Medicine Reviews*.)
- **Irish, Kline, Gunn, Buysse & Hall (2015).** "The role of sleep hygiene in promoting public health: A review of empirical evidence." *Sleep Med Rev* 22:23–36. [S] The evidence behind individual hygiene rules is mixed.
- **Implication:** Don't sell "tips." Locturne's value is behavioural enforcement (stimulus control), not education.

**Digital CBT-I at scale**
- **Freeman, Sheaves, Goodwin, … Espie (2017).** "The effects of improving sleep on mental health (OASIS): a randomised controlled trial with mediation analysis." *Lancet Psychiatry* 4(10):749–758. DOI 10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30328-0. [S] N = 3,755 students at 26 UK universities. Digital CBT-I reduced insomnia, paranoia and hallucinations, with insomnia as the mediator.
- **Implication:** Fully automated sleep interventions can work at scale in students, which is Locturne's demographic.

**Consistent wake time and sleep regularity**
- **Windred, Burns, Lane, … Phillips (2024).** "Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration: A prospective cohort study." *Sleep* 47(1):zsad253. https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/47/1/zsad253/7280269 [S] About 60,000 UK Biobank participants with accelerometry. The most regular quintile had about 30% lower all-cause mortality than the least regular. Observational.
- **Phillips, Clerx, O'Brien, … Klerman (2017).** "Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing." *Sci Rep* 7:3216. DOI 10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4. [S] N = 61 Harvard undergraduates. Sleep regularity correlated with GPA (r = 0.37). Irregular sleepers had later melatonin onset.
- **Implication:** A fixed wake anchor enforced by "apps sleep until you're up and moving" targets regularity, which may matter more than total hours. The evidence is observational, so market it as "consistency," not as a mortality claim.

**Morning light and circadian anchoring**
- **Wright, McHill, Birks, Griffin, Rusterholz & Chinoy (2013).** "Entrainment of the human circadian clock to the natural light-dark cycle." *Current Biology* 23(16):1554–1558. https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(13)00764-1 [S] A week of camping advanced the clock by aligning it to natural light. Melatonin onset moved to about 2 hours before sleep.
- **Burns, Saxena, Vetter, Phillips, Lane & Cain (2021).** "Time spent in outdoor light is associated with mood, sleep, and circadian rhythm-related outcomes: A cross-sectional and longitudinal study in over 400,000 UK Biobank participants." *J Affect Disord* 295:347–352. [S] Each extra hour of daylight outdoors was associated with lower depression risk and better sleep. Observational.
- **Brown, Brainard, Cajochen, … Wright (2022).** "Recommendations for daytime, evening, and nighttime indoor light exposure to best support physiology, sleep, and wakefulness in healthy adults." *PLOS Biol* 20(3):e3001571. DOI 10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571. [S] Expert consensus:
  - At least 250 lux melanopic EDI during the day.
  - At most 10 lux from 3 hours before bed.
  - As dark as possible during sleep.
- **Richardson, Micic, Cain, Bartel, Maddock & Gradisar (2018).** "A randomised controlled trial of bright light therapy and morning activity for adolescents and young adults with delayed sleep-wake phase disorder." *Sleep Medicine* 45:114–123. [S]
  - Morning light plus **morning activity** improved sleep timing, sleep onset latency and daytime functioning, maintained at three months.
  - Long-term relapse was common.
  - There was no difference between short- and long-wavelength light.
- **Implication:** Making the 200-step walk an outdoor morning walk would add a circadian-anchoring mechanism with decent supporting evidence. The DSWPD trial pairing light with morning activity is the closest analogue to Locturne's morning gate.

## 5. Morning: getting up, sleep inertia, snoozing, alarms

**Sleep inertia**
- **Hilditch & McHill (2019).** "Sleep inertia: current insights." *Nat Sci Sleep* 11:155–165. DOI 10.2147/NSS.S188911. [A] Grogginess and cognitive impairment on waking fade with time awake and are worse after sleep loss. The review covers countermeasures such as light, sound and caffeine; I could not extract its strength ratings for exercise.
- **Implication:** Expect users to be impaired for the first 15–30 minutes or so. Keep the morning task simple and physical, not cognitive.

**Snoozing: mixed evidence**
- **Sundelin, Landry & Axelsson (2024).** "Is snoozing losing? Why intermittent morning alarms are used and how they affect sleep, cognition, cortisol, and mood." *J Sleep Res* 33(3):e14054. DOI 10.1111/jsr.14054. [A]
  - Survey study, n = 1,732: snoozing is common, especially in younger people and later chronotypes.
  - Laboratory study, n = 31 habitual snoozers with polysomnography: 30 minutes of snoozing improved or did not affect cognition on rising, cost about 6 minutes of sleep, and had no clear effect on cortisol, mood or sleepiness.
- **Ogawa, Kaizuma-Ueyama & Hayashi (2022).** "Effects of using a snooze alarm on sleep inertia after morning awakening." *J Physiol Anthropol*. PMC9804954. [FT] Survey n = 293 plus a laboratory study n = 10. Snooze produced more fragmented, lighter sleep before waking and slower reaction times. Vigour improved only without snooze.
- **Mattingly, Martinez, Young, Cain & Striegel (2022).** "Snoozing: an examination of a common method of waking." *Sleep* 45(10):zsac184. [FT] N = 450 professionals, observational. 57% were habitual snoozers. Snoozing was associated with eveningness, lighter sleep before waking and fewer steps. There was no difference in sleep duration.
- **Implication:** The evidence does not support framing snoozing as harmful. Locturne's gate is about getting out of bed and into the day, not about banning a brief snooze. Allow a short grace period.

**Wake-up tasks**
- **Oh, Ko, Shin & Ko (2022).** "Using wake-up tasks for morning behavior change: Development and usability study." *JMIR Formative Research*. PMC9529170. [FT]
  - N = 36, two weeks, between-group. Alarms dismissed by button, math problem, or photo-matching, with a target of 15 squats within 10 minutes.
  - The photo task had a 94.2% early success rate versus 75.8% for the button. Squats started about 84 seconds after the alarm versus about 334 seconds.
  - A single-site pilot.
- **Implication:** This is the closest direct evidence for Locturne's morning gate. A physical dismissal task tied to a target behaviour increases follow-through. It is weak but on-target.

**Alarm across the room**
- I found **no peer-reviewed trial**. Only expert advice, patents and products (for example Clocky). **[U]**
- **Implication:** Present step-gating as plausible and supported by related evidence (wake-up tasks, stimulus control, morning activity), not as proven.

## 6. Engagement and attrition in digital health apps

**Baumel, Muench, Edan & Kane (2019).** "Objective user engagement with mental health apps: Systematic search and panel-based usage analysis." *JMIR* 21(9):e14567. DOI 10.2196/14567. [A]
- 93 Android apps with at least 10,000 installs.
- Median daily open rate 4.0%.
- **Median 15-day retention 3.9%; 30-day retention 3.3%.** (The brief's 3.9% is the 15-day figure.)
- Trackers (6.1%), peer support (8.9%) and mindfulness (4.7%) apps retained better at day 30 than breathing apps (0%).
- Use peaked in the evening.

**Torous, Lipschitz, Ng & Firth (2020).** "Dropout rates in clinical trials of smartphone apps for depressive symptoms: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *J Affect Disord* 263:413–419. DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2019.11.167. [A]
- 18 RCTs, N = 3,336.
- Pooled dropout 26.2%, or **47.8% after adjusting for publication bias**.
- Dropout was lower with human feedback and in-app mood monitoring, and higher in larger studies.

**Eysenbach (2005).** "The law of attrition." *JMIR* 7(1):e11. DOI 10.2196/jmir.7.1.e11. [A] Substantial dropout is the norm in eHealth.

**Implications**
- Assume most users disappear within 30 days.
- A background blocker that works without the app being opened is structurally better placed than engagement-dependent apps.
- Self-monitoring and some human or social touch predict retention.
- Rotating features increases uninstalls unless explained (Kovacs 2018).
- For a hard paywall this cuts both ways: the value must show up in the first week.

## 7. Risks of these interventions

**Orthosomnia**
- **Baron, Abbott, Jao, Manalo & Mullen (2017).** "Orthosomnia: Are some patients taking the quantified self too far?" *J Clin Sleep Med* 13(2):351–354. DOI 10.5664/jcsm.6472. [A] A case series, not a trial. Patients' perfectionist chasing of sleep-tracker data worsened insomnia.
- **Implication:** Don't show sleep-stage or "sleep score" data. Track behaviour (apps asleep on time, walked in the morning), not sleep quality.

**Abstinence-violation and "what-the-hell" effects**
- Marlatt & Gordon (1985), *Relapse Prevention* (book). Cochran & Tesser (1996), "The 'what the hell' effect: Some effects of goal proximity and goal framing on performance" (book chapter). [S]
- A single lapse framed as total failure triggers full relapse. Silverman & Barasch (2023) show the same pattern for broken streaks.
- **Implication:** Frame misses as normal (Lally: one miss is harmless). Offer repair, and reward a return after a miss (Milkman 2021).

**Stress and goal inflation from rigid rules**
- GoalKeeper (above): the strong lockout produced the highest stress, and users loosened goals "just in case."
- Castelo 2025: only 25.5% complied fully with a total internet block.
- Kovacs 2021: users drift toward weaker regimens when allowed.

**Reactance**
- The classic theory is Brehm's reactance theory. I found no RCT specific to sleep blockers. Evidence that imposed tools trigger rejection is qualitative [S; HCI user-review studies].
- **Implication:** A self-chosen commitment made in advance (Allcott 2022) avoids most reactance. The risk comes from rules that feel imposed or unexplained.

**Evidence quality, as a risk in itself**
- Radtke, Apel, Schenkel, Keller & von Lindern (2022). "Digital detox: An effective solution in the smartphone era? A systematic literature review." *Mobile Media & Communication* 10(2):190–215. DOI 10.1177/20501579211028647. [S] Detox effects are mixed across studies.
- Most smartphone and sleep trials are small, short, self-reported and in students.
- **Implication:** Keep marketing claims modest: "about 15–20 more minutes of sleep" is the defensible range.

## 8. Autonomy-supportive design and strict versus flexible blocking

**Self-determination theory**
- **Ryan & Deci (2000).** "Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being." *Am Psychol* 55(1):68–78. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68. [A] Three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness.
- **Ntoumanis, Ng, Prestwich, … Williams (2021).** "A meta-analysis of self-determination theory-informed intervention studies in the health domain." *Health Psychol Rev* 15(2):214–244. DOI 10.1080/17437199.2020.1718529. [A] 73 studies. Small-to-medium effects on health behaviour, maintained at follow-up. Gains in need support and autonomous motivation, but not controlled motivation, predicted behaviour change.
- **Peters, Calvo & Ryan (2018).** "Designing for motivation, engagement and wellbeing in digital experience." *Front Psychol* 9:797. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00797. [S] The METUX framework for applying SDT to technology design.

**Synthesis on strict versus flexible**
- **Hard limits reduce use most:**
  - GoalKeeper: strong lockout −73.7 min/day (d 0.54), versus weak lockout −50.4 min/day (d 0.35).
  - Allcott 2022: 22 min/day, durable over 12 weeks.
  - Castelo 2025: large wellbeing effects.
- **Their costs:** stress, looser goals, and low compliance or opt-out.
- **What works best is a hybrid:**
  1. **Self-chosen in advance** (autonomy): the user sets the schedule and the app list.
  2. **Enforced in the moment** (commitment): settings changes take effect tomorrow, and overrides are rare.
  3. **Escape valves with friction, not a free pass:** a one sec-style delay plus a dismiss option, and emergency access.
  4. **Weak-to-middle strength as the default**, which 52.8% of GoalKeeper users preferred, with a strict option for the minority who want it (33.3% preferred strong).

**Bottom line for Locturne.** The best-supported combination is:
- a self-set, enforced block of engaging apps starting 30–60 minutes before bed (commitment device plus stimulus control);
- a fixed morning release tied to a simple physical action, ideally outdoors (regularity plus light plus wake-up task);
- if-then planning at onboarding;
- lapse-tolerant tracking of behaviour, not sleep scores;
- a moderate default strictness with a friction-based override instead of a free pass.

A realistic claimed effect is about 15–25 more minutes of sleep and earlier lights-out, with larger effects on wellbeing and use than on sleep architecture.

**Gaps and unverified items**
- Allcott 2020's sample size and wellbeing effect size, the pooled effects in Monge Roffarello & De Russis 2023, Hughes & Burke's sleep statistics, Patel 2016's exact percentages, and Wellspent's authors.
- No peer-reviewed evidence on alarms placed across the room, or on Apple Screen Time's effectiveness specifically.
- The Allcott et al. analysis of snooze delays was announced but I could not find it published.
