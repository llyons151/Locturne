## The Post-Attribution Playbook for Growth (Eric Seufert, Mobile Dev Memo / Fabulous, 2025-09-03, https://youtu.be/0dZ-wwsNLWY)
- **Relevance:** low. The episode is about UA measurement, AI in marketing and channel strategy.
- **Findings:**
  - "Signal engineering": you can add a deliberate hurdle before the product, such as a pointless captcha, to screen for high-intent users, then send completion to the ad platform as the optimization event.   - Other topics (one line): use gen-AI for creative concepting, not for variants. Don't try to explain why an ad won. Fill the biggest channel first ("waterfall") rather than diversifying for its own sake. Kill losing creatives fast. Measurement disorganization is the biggest growth-team failure.

## Value-Driven Growth: LinkedIn's Billion-Dollar Subscription Strategy (Ora Levit, LinkedIn Premium, 2025-09-17, https://youtu.be/CdlajgDk_tw)
- **Relevance:** medium. It covers personalized paywalls by intent, retention through plan structure, and win-back with a second free trial.
- **Findings:**
  - LinkedIn Premium runs 1,000+ A/B tests a year on the premium team alone. Everything shipped is tested: features, design and value copy. Evidence: observed practice.
  - Personalization by intent: the premium flow asks one question (job seeking, growing a business, hiring or other), combines it with recent behavior such as a job search, and shows matching content and plans. They say they have found no downside to personalization; the downside comes from doing too little of it. Evidence: opinion backed by testing culture.
  - Messaging alone improved retention. Explaining what benefits exist and how to use them lifted retention even with no product change. Evidence: observed, no numbers.
  - Their health metric is the "premium session": a session where a paying member touches a premium feature. Frequent reach for the feature is read as a signal of long-term retention.
  - They judge launches on annualized bookings and model pricing experiments over a 5-year horizon. They explicitly decline revenue they think damages the funnel long term.
  - Plan structure for retention: a big annual discount, plus a "dual plan" (you plus a partner, friend or colleague) at 20% off. Both retain better, attributed partly to social effects and partly to value per dollar. Evidence: observed, no numbers.
  - Premium feature pattern: give a free taste and charge for the enhanced version. Example: one cover photo is free, rotating cover photos are paid.
  - A "Top Choice" job flag, capped at 3 a month (artificial scarcity), gives a 46% higher chance of a recruiter reply. Evidence: observed data.
  - Win-back ("boomerangs"): a growing share of new subscriptions come from returning lapsed subscribers. Prior subscription history is used to decide messaging and whether to show a win-back offer. They avoid broad coupon discounting ("our price is what it is"); the main discount is the annual plan.
  - A second free trial for lapsed users is offered after 12 months, on the grounds that the product has changed since they last tried it. Still being tested. The host notes that App Store rules make this hard: you have to use offers.
  - Rotating partner perks (Notion AI, Spotify, Headspace, Duolingo) sit in a benefits panel and are refreshed every 6 to 12 months. Members who use them retain better.
- **Tactics:** an intent question at the paywall entry that routes to a tailored plan; annual discount; 2-person plan at 20% off; a win-back framed as "5 new things since you left"; a second trial after 12 months.
- **Caveats:** LinkedIn has huge free engagement and a network effect. The second-trial tactic is still in testing. The translation artifacts make exact wording unreliable.

## How Condé Nast Experiments, Bundles, and Wins (Michael Ribeiro, Condé Nast, 2025-10-29, https://youtu.be/0qt-wHxiFMY)
- **Relevance:** medium. It covers post-purchase upsell, low-propensity offers, walking back a paywall, and tiers.
- **Findings:**
  - Post-purchase upsell: right after someone subscribes (for example to Architectural Digest), show one pop-up offering a higher-tier business membership. About 5% convert, adding roughly $100 to $200 of LTV at zero extra acquisition cost. Evidence: observed data. The purchase moment is when the customer is most engaged.
  - At the point of purchase, "more for less" wins. From his time in TV: people watch 6 to 8 channels, but "get 100+ channels" beat every "just the channels you watch" framing in every test. Evidence: A/B tests, no numbers given.
  - Low-propensity offer: at the Washington Post, a hidden "starter pack" (about $2/month for 3 to 4 stories) was shown only to users predicted to have low propensity, based on referral source. The goal was to get them into the payment system and upgrade later. LTV verdict is "still out." Evidence: observed.
  - Walking back: a registration wall on Pitchfork drew backlash, so they removed it and plan to retry later. He argues admitting a mistake earns goodwill.
  - Premium tiers: adoption of new higher tiers has been low single-digit percent in his experience. His default is not to launch a premium tier until someone proves the cohort is bigger than 1 to 2%. Grow core subscription value and retention first. Evidence: observed plus opinion.
  - Internal bundling: fold existing extras (for example Wired editor AMAs) into the subscription as benefits. Low effort.
- **Caveats:** this is a media and publishing context. His tier skepticism runs against the Tinder episodes in this same batch.
- **Quote:** "When you have that moment of truth... put your best foot forward."

## How Tinder Captures More Value With Tiered Pricing and Consumables (Ravi Mehta, ex-Tinder CPO, 2025-11-26, https://youtu.be/AWI3rofh0BA)
- **Relevance:** high. It covers onboarding length, the aha moment, trial model choice, price anchoring and tiers.
- **Findings:**
  - Longer checkout converted better. Sesame Care (cash-pay telehealth, GLP-1 weight loss) replaced a 3-screen checkout with a roughly 20 to 25 question intake: estimated weight loss, medical history, motivations, what you've tried. Conversion rose 40%, and step-to-step completion was above 99%. The mechanism is that each question built confidence that the care was thorough and not a "pill mill." Evidence: observed before/after, not a clean A/B test.
  - Shorter onboarding also works. Tinder cut dating-site onboarding from 20 to 30 minutes to about 1.5 to 2 minutes (Facebook login, pull photos, short bio). That expanded the whole online-dating TAM to people with low willingness to spend time. Evidence: observed.
  - Choose onboarding length from the aha moment. For GLP-1 the aha is the doctor visit, so front-load information gathering. For dating it is the first conversation, so get there fast. Pick one extreme and avoid the "messy middle." Evidence: framework/opinion.
  - Psych framework (Darius Contractor): each screen adds or removes motivation. Email form fills cost about 5 points and credit card entry about 10. Password creation is one of the biggest motivation killers, so use phone OTP or Sign in with Apple/Google instead. Evidence: framework.
  - If onboarding delivers value, it can be any length ("90 screens"). Calm plays rain sounds from the first open and even in its ads, delivering the promise before any intake. An onboarding that feels like an "IRS form" makes people bounce. Evidence: opinion with examples.
  - Personalization can backfire when labeled. At TripAdvisor, a personalized hotel sort converted better silently, but conversion dropped once it was branded "Just for you." Travelers didn't trust the site to decide for them. For high-consequence decisions, users need to feel they reached the choice themselves. Evidence: A/B test, direction only.
  - Choosing a trial model: free-to-play if you have network effects. A limited paid trial (about 7 days) if you can build the habit loop fast. Pay upfront, with no trial, if financial commitment drives adoption. His example is gyms: you'd never give a 7-day free trial, because paying creates the personal commitment to show up. Evidence: framework.
  - Anchor high. Someone who installed is already motivated. Start around $20 to $30/month (whatever the category allows) and optimize down. If nobody pays that, it's a product-market-fit problem, not a pricing one. Founders under-monetize: Granola's free tier never converted him, while Wispr Flow's limited trial did. Evidence: opinion plus anecdote.
  - Price against the whole system the product sits in: heavy Tinder spenders justified $100 to $200/month against roughly $400/month spent on dates. Evidence: user interviews.
  - Tiers: about 85 to 90% of Tinder users are free. Two or three subscription tiers is the most before it gets confusing, and consumables fill the gaps. Almost no free users buy consumables; subscriptions act as a force multiplier for them. The cheap tier is an on-ramp. Platinum came from whales spending $100 to $250/month on consumables, and research showed the motive was utility, not status. Super Likes make a match 3x more likely. Evidence: observed data.
  - Tinder once had hundreds of App Store SKUs from price testing by tier, bundle and country.
  - Owned audience: turn earned reach into email and product habits, because algorithms (TikTok, YouTube) no longer guarantee distribution to your followers.
- **Tactics:** phone-OTP sign-up; deliver the product's promise during onboarding; the gym logic of paying upfront to create commitment; a high first price point.
- **Caveats:** the Sesame result is a V1 vs V2 program change, not an isolated A/B test. The gym analogy runs against the common "always offer a trial" advice.
- **Quote:** "Sometimes the best way to optimize your intake... is not to simplify it, but to extend it around the psychology of the user."

## Pivots, Funding, and Building Apps That Last (Greg Cohn, Ad Hoc Labs / Burner, 2025-12-10, https://youtu.be/PSu9ZrwKHLo)
- **Relevance:** high. It covers the optional free trial, trial lengths, tourists vs locals, and win-back at the end of the lifecycle.
- **Findings:**
  - Making the free trial optional instead of the default was a "very positive" test (no number given). The idea came from the trial-toggle discussion in the app-growth community. Their reason it's authentic: some high-intent users want to lock in and pay now, while skeptics want the trial. Letting people self-select serves both. Evidence: A/B test, no numbers.
  - What you sell someone at the paywall and onboarding is the single biggest determinant of their retention, according to Cohn.
  - They run both a 3-day and a 7-day trial treatment and bucket users by cohort. The north star is cohort LTV, projected from day-8 and day-30 signals.
  - Tourists vs locals: many users need a number briefly (a Craigslist sale), others indefinitely. So far segmentation happens by self-selection across SKUs and bundles (monthly or annual × 1 or 3 lines × standard or premium = 8 SKUs, plus credits), not by prediction. 20 to 30% of new subscriptions come from returning former subscribers (the "smile curve"). Evidence: observed data.
  - End-of-life moment as win-back: burning a number is a fun, animated moment, and it's used to offer a new number, which retains a share of users. Evidence: observed.
  - Launching subscriptions (unlimited texts and calls plus MMS, monthly and annual) was the biggest single step change in revenue. Before that it was a $1.99 paid download with credits, which made about $50k on day one. Evidence: observed.
  - In hindsight, forcing everyone into subscriptions and deprecating credits raised churn. Keeping a credit option for high short-term, low long-term intent users might have fit the demand curve better. Evidence: reflection.
  - Paywall test velocity went from 1 to 2 tests a month to a multiple of that after adopting tooling. About 1 in 4 or 5 tests wins. "That first hour of the user journey is just incredibly important." The next targets are onboarding and cancellation flows.
  - Mainstream press traffic (such as a Vogue mention) doesn't convert or retain. High-intent search traffic does. Evidence: observed.
  - Premium features aimed at "locals" (caller ID lookup, AI voicemail, VPN) drive upsell and retention, but can take a long time to show up in paywall conversion. Standalone sister apps failed to get traction; adding features inside the flagship app worked better.
  - Revenue path: $1M in year about 2.5, $5M by year about 5, $10M after a couple more years, now a multiple of that. About 30 staff.
- **Caveats:** Burner is churn-biased by nature ("don't start with an inherently temporary product").
- **Quote:** "If you're not thinking about bundling, you should be."

## Creative Testing, Data-Driven Decisions, and Growing Mobile Apps (Alper Taner, stealth app studio, 2025-12-24, https://youtu.be/FF2GKgBi6ZI)
- **Relevance:** low-medium. It is mostly UA, but has one point on reading funnel metrics.
- **Findings:**
  - A low install-to-trial rate (for example 5% against a health/fitness benchmark of 15 to 20%) is "a fact, not a problem" until you've tested systematically. Cheap installs or App Store features naturally convert worse (Barnard: featured cohorts convert poorly). Look at the whole funnel, not one metric. Evidence: opinion.
  - Don't copy competitor paywalls or web funnels one-to-one. Treat them as inspiration, because audience, pricing and promise differ. Many health/fitness web funnels are now near-identical copies. Evidence: opinion.
  - Mapping the trial event to "Trial" instead of "Purchase" on Meta cut cost per trial by about 35% with the same creatives. The reasoning is to send honest, funnel-logical signals rather than compete in auctions with $200-AOV ecommerce buyers. Evidence: observed test.
- **Other (one line):** test with a hypothesis; judge creative programs on success rate, not volume; give under-delivering ads their own ad set; the cap-inflated budget trick; incrementality testing above about $500k/month.

## From Tinder to VC: Product-Market Fit, Monetization, AI-Driven Growth (Jeff Morris, ex-Tinder VP Product/Revenue, Chapter One, 2026-01-07, https://youtu.be/5qWhNLUR3MA)
- **Relevance:** high. It covers paywall placement, the hard-paywall false signal, reaching the aha moment before asking for money, and paywall design.
- **Findings:**
  - A top-nav paywall entry point earned $50M. In 2018 Tinder tested a toggle in the top nav bar that opened a new subscription tier. It was treated as a small throwaway A/B test and produced about $50M in revenue. Design had resisted the top nav. Lesson: paywall entry points are "real estate," and teams are too timid about placement. Evidence: A/B test.
  - Every subscription feature at Tinder had its own paywall entry point, tracked daily for taps, conversion and incremental revenue. A power law applies: 1 to 3 features drive most new subscriptions. Features whose paywall didn't convert were made free. They never moved a free feature to paid. Evidence: observed practice.
  - Hard paywalls can fake product-market fit. People pay to try, then churn, and with annual plans you won't see how bad retention is for 12 months. Tinder waited until users matched and chatted before asking for money. Evidence: opinion.
  - Don't ask for a credit card before the user has had a successful outcome. Example: an AI website builder whose paying users hit buggy first builds, which caused frustration on Twitter. Evidence: anecdote.
  - Swipe limits were introduced for ecosystem health (auto-swipers, people swiping through a whole city) and turned out to be great for monetization. Frame paid features as "superpowers" that can't be given to everyone.
  - Paywall craft: they gave paywalls as much design love as core features. A small shimmer animation added in 2017 cost a couple of days of engineering. Many paywalls "look like they were designed by a PM." Evidence: anecdote.
  - Beware over-monetization in mature products: too many paywalls make a product feel spammy and "grind" users.
  - A portfolio AI prosumer app hit about $50M ARR (?) with no paywall or pricing optimization at all. His first moves would be packaging (multiple tiers, entry-level to whale) and geographic price localization. Tinder had whales spending up to about $50,000/year (?).
  - Users tolerate monetization changes more than teams fear if the core value stays and the change is transparent and fair.
  - Flighty (via host): the first flight is free with all pro features, after which features lock. It's a usage-limited taste for a high-cost product. Evidence: observed.
- **Caveats:** Tinder had tens of millions of MAU, and he says a top-nav entry might not work in, for example, a meditation app. The $50k/year whale figure may be a caption error.
- **Quote:** "Revenue teams should be... a bit more aggressive in terms of pricing and entry points."

## Why Your Free Users Are Your Real Growth Engine (Cem Kansu, Duolingo CPO, 2026-01-21, https://youtu.be/pqbbyRMssbQ)
- **Relevance:** high. It covers trial reminders, button copy, freemium vs the "premium trap," streak monetization and ad placement.
- **Findings:**
  - Button copy had a large effect: "Try for $0.00" beat "Try for free," which beat "Start free trial," which beat the original "Subscribe." $0.00 lifted both trial starts and trial-to-paid. The reasoning is that people react emotionally on mobile rather than reading a logical value exchange. Evidence: A/B test, direction only, no numbers.
  - Free trials helped even in a freemium app. The CPO resisted them ("the product is already free") and was wrong. Trials lower the barrier for skeptics even when paid features don't need "trying" (ad-free, offline). Evidence: A/B test.
  - Trial transparency beats subscription aversion. The checkout explains the trial mechanics ("7-day free trial, today is day 5, we'll notify you before you're charged"). This counters subscription aversion and fear of scams, which is stronger outside the US. Evidence: A/B test.
  - First-ever monetization was a $1 to $3 in-app purchase to repair a broken streak. A meaningful share paid, showing users care deeply about their streaks. Evidence: observed.
  - The first subscription was validated with an odd one-time purchase of 30 days ad-free at $3, $5 or $10. It did "much better than expected," so they built Plus: no ads, offline, and "support our mission" as a third benefit. Many early buyers paid mainly to support the mission. Evidence: test.
  - A big subscription turning point was moving an existing pacing mechanic ("hearts," a mistake limit) into the subscription as unlimited hearts, because many users want new material rather than repetition.
  - Ads appear only after a lesson ends, never before or during. Ad tests were mostly retention-neutral. Complaint volume was quantified per million DAU, and "no Finnish course" complaints outnumbered ad complaints 3 to 1. Evidence: observed.
  - Subscription promos are shown in ad slots, chosen by an ML model optimizing LTV, with frequency backoff after about 3 ignored impressions. The host recommends ad-supported apps promote their own premium tier this way.
  - About 10% of MAU pay, and subscriptions are 80%+ of revenue. About 400 A/B tests run at once, many in the purchase flow.
  - The "premium trap": moving free features behind the paywall turns metrics green for 6 to 12 months, then stops working and invites a free competitor. They are moving features from paid to free: Practice Hub, and AI answer explanations from Max once LLM costs fell. They accept a measured revenue cut because free users drive word of mouth, the main growth engine. Evidence: A/B test plus strategy.
  - Long control groups (3 to 6 months) are used for things with different short- and long-term effects. Example: a new push notification gives a novelty bump, but after about 6 weeks metrics can turn negative from over-notification. Evidence: observed.
  - The number one competitor is screen time generally (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube), not other education apps.
  - "People don't read": design must guide the user, and if you need text to explain a screen, you've lost. Don't build for a single persona when there are many reasons people use the product.
  - Failure: human tutors on demand were heavily requested but nobody used them, because speaking to a live person was too scary. Don't treat user requests as truth.
  - Standalone apps (Math) were folded back into the main app, because cross-promoting to existing users is far easier than new-app acquisition.
- **Tactics:** "$0.00" trial button; trial timeline showing "today is day 5 of 7, we'll remind you"; paid repair of a broken streak; post-session-only ad slot; paid feature that removes a friction mechanic (unlimited hearts).
- **Caveats:** freemium at massive scale with network-like word of mouth. The CPO says copying Duolingo's flows "probably won't work for most apps." The transcript is machine-translated, so the exact button strings are uncertain.
- **Quote:** "People don't perceive mobile interfaces that way at all. It's... more based on emotion and reaction."

## Batch-level patterns
- **Paywall entry points and copy are high-leverage and cheap to test.** Tinder's top-nav toggle made about $50M, Duolingo's "$0.00" button lifted both trial starts and conversion, and Burner multiplied its paywall test velocity with about 1 in 4 to 5 tests winning. Placement and microcopy are where the easy wins are.
- **Onboarding length should follow the aha moment, not a rule.** Sesame's longer intake raised conversion 40% because the questions built confidence, while Tinder's roughly 2-minute onboarding expanded the market. Both Mehta and Duolingo stress that users react emotionally, not logically. Onboarding that delivers the promise during setup (Calm's rain sounds) can run long.
- **Trials help even when "unnecessary," and transparency helps them work.** Duolingo found trials useful in a freemium app, and a "today is day 5, we'll remind you" timeline reduced subscription aversion. Burner's optional (not default) trial won because high-intent users could pay immediately. Mehta's gym logic says paying upfront sometimes creates the commitment that drives use.
- **Hard paywalls give a false read on product-market fit.** Morris, Barnard and Duolingo's "premium trap" all warn that upfront monetization or taking value from free users makes metrics look good for 6 to 12 months while retention and word of mouth erode. Several guests say to judge on cohort LTV and retention, not early revenue.
- **Win-back is a real growth line.** LinkedIn's "boomerangs" (plus a second free trial after 12 months), Burner's 20 to 30% returning subscribers and number-burn re-offer, and Crowley's "make cancelling easy, then remarket" all treat churned users as a warm pool.
- **Personalize, but don't announce it.** LinkedIn's one-question intent routing works. TripAdvisor's "Just for you" label lowered conversion on a high-stakes choice. Tailor quietly and let users feel they chose.
- **Tiers and consumables fill the demand curve, but the evidence splits.** The Tinder guests say a high first price, 2 to 3 tiers and consumables for whales, with consumables bought almost only by subscribers. Condé Nast says new premium tiers get low single-digit adoption, so strengthen the core plan first. Post-purchase upsells (about 5% take rate) are the lowest-risk extra revenue.
- **Screen-time blocking is seen as a growing, investable category.** Two guests (BlueThrone, GP Bullhound) name it a category-killer opportunity, cite Opal's ~$120/year pricing framed as productivity ROI for knowledge workers, and note that the category grows with each entrant and isn't blocked by Apple's built-in Screen Time.
- **Commitment and streak psychology monetize.** Duolingo's first revenue was paid streak repair. LinkedIn's scarce "Top Choice" flag (3 a month) raised recruiter replies 46%. Tinder's swipe limits protected the ecosystem and sold subscriptions. Limits framed as "superpowers" beat plain feature gates.
